# Supply and Demand Elasticity

## The idea of elasticity

Elasticity measures how strongly one quantity responds to a change in another. In markets we usually ask how much the quantity demanded or supplied changes when price changes, measured in percentage terms so that units do not matter.

## Key terms

- **Price elasticity of demand**: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.
- **Elastic demand**: demand whose elasticity has an absolute value greater than one, so quantity responds more than proportionally to price.
- **Inelastic demand**: demand whose elasticity has an absolute value less than one, so quantity responds less than proportionally to price.
- **Unit elastic**: the case where the absolute elasticity equals exactly one and total revenue does not change when price changes.
- **Income elasticity**: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in consumer income.
- **Cross-price elasticity**: the percentage change in quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good.

## What makes demand elastic

Demand tends to be more elastic when close substitutes exist, when the good takes a large share of the budget, and when buyers have more time to adjust. Necessities such as basic medicine tend to be inelastic.

## Elasticity and total revenue

When demand is elastic, cutting the price raises total revenue because quantity rises by a larger percentage. When demand is inelastic, raising the price raises total revenue. Firms and governments use this to predict how sales taxes or discounts change spending.

## Supply side

- **Price elasticity of supply**: the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price.

Supply is usually more elastic in the long run, because producers can build capacity, hire staff, and enter or leave the market.
